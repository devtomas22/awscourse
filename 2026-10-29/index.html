Main concepts

Key AWS cost drivers
The five categories that make up an AWS bill: compute (EC2, Lambda, container services), storage (S3, EBS, EFS), databases (RDS, DynamoDB), data transfer (out of AWS, across regions), and networking (VPC configuration, NAT gateways, load balancers). Which one dominates spend depends on what you run — and how well it is configured and monitored.
Compute costs
Accumulate by usage hours and instance type: EC2 bills per instance-hour, so high usage or premium instance families drive expenses up quickly. Lambda bills per request plus a GB-second charge for execution time; container services (AWS Fargate) bill per vCPU-hour and per GB-hour.
Storage costs
Depend on the amount stored, access frequency, and storage class. Frequent data retrieval, long-term storage, and high-performance tiers increase spending. Lifecycle rules and Intelligent-Tiering move rarely-read objects into cheaper classes automatically — the class/access split is the lever.
Database costs
Driven by instance size and the number of read and write operations — RDS by the instance scale (engine and storage on top), DynamoDB by capacity units. Backups and additional features like multi-AZ or extra availability boost overall spend.
Data transfer costs
Come from moving data out of AWS or across regions. Data transfer IN (ingress) is free; internet egress is billed per GB beyond the 100 GB/month free allowance aggregated across services and regions (except China and GovCloud); inter-region routes charge on both ends. Large-scale data movement can lead to significant charges — monitoring helps avoid them — and serving users through CloudFront can cut egress cost.
Networking costs
VPC configuration, NAT gateways and load balancers are impacted by traffic flow and service configuration — a NAT gateway bills hourly plus per GB processed, and load balancers bill for their hours plus a capacity component. Misconfigured or under-monitored networking can silently inflate the bill, which is why monitoring is critical for cost control in this category especially.
Why monitoring matters
It gives real-time insight into resource usage and spending: detect usage spikes and anomalies before they lead to high costs, identify idle or underutilized resources to eliminate waste, adjust provisioning in response to actual demand, set up alerts to respond quickly to unexpected spending, and inform budgeting and capacity planning with accurate data. Together these give managers and technical teams one shared, accurate view of cloud spend.
The three pricing options
Free tier for limited free usage, prepaid commitments — Reserved Instances and Savings Plans — with discounts for long-term commitment, and postpaid on-demand billing based on actual usage. Commitments trade flexibility for the lowest unit price; on-demand is the reverse; free tier exists to make experimentation cheap.
The free tier's three shapes
12-months free: free usage for the first 12 months after account creation on popular services — for example 750 hours per month of EC2 micro instances and limited S3 storage. Always free: certain offerings stay free indefinitely, within defined limits. Short-term trials: limited-time free trials so you can test a feature before it starts charging.
Reserved Instances
Commit to running specific EC2 instance types for 1 or 3 years in exchange for lower hourly rates — up to 72% off on-demand. The highest fixed-shape discount, but the least flexibility in changing instance attributes, which makes them the natural pick for predictable, steady, long-term workloads. Standard RIs fix the shape; Convertible RIs trade some discount for the ability to swap instance attributes later.
Savings Plans
Commit to a consistent dollar-per-hour of usage for 1 or 3 years for savings up to 72% — slightly less than Reserved Instances' maximum but with far more flexibility: Compute Savings Plans apply across EC2, Fargate and Lambda regardless of instance family, size, OS, tenancy or Region. The more flexible default for the majority of workloads and business cases.
On-demand
Pay-as-you-go billing on actual usage — per instance-hour, per gigabyte stored, or per request. Highest flexibility but the most expensive per unit; best for variable and unpredictable workloads where you scale up or down quickly without overcommitting — startups and projects with fluctuating requirements especially.
Spot Instances
AWS's spare EC2 capacity at up to 90% off on-demand prices. The catch: AWS can reclaim it with a 2-minute interruption notice. Fits fault-tolerant, interruptible workloads — big data, CI/CD, HPC, rendering — layered on top of the commitments and on-demand trio.
Tiered pricing and hidden fees
Some services use tiered pricing: the per-unit cost decreases as usage levels rise, making larger deployments cheaper per GB. But some costs carry hidden fees — API requests, backups and snapshots, data transfer between AWS regions, or licensing. Reviewing the pricing details (or estimating first with the AWS Pricing Calculator) is essential before committing.
AWS Cost Explorer
Visualizes spending trends over time — a monthly chart stacked by cost category makes changes obvious — and breaks down costs by service, region, and tag, isolating the biggest drivers and inefficiencies quickly. supports forecasts and anomaly spotting for budgeting. User-defined tags must be activated as cost-allocation tags before they appear in these breakdowns.
Billing dashboards and alerts
The Billing console gives a consolidated view of current charges organized by service with usage metrics and costs, detailed billing data and cost allocation by service, region or tags, and deeper breakdowns by region, instance type and usage type to pinpoint which workloads drive higher costs. Alerts: choose a static threshold (a trigger like greater-than a specified value) or anomaly detection; when usage crosses it, AWS sends a notification — surprise bills get caught in real time.
Five provisioning decisions
The deployments that shape long-term cost: region selection (regions price differently — align with your user base and budget to avoid unnecessary data transfer and compute premiums); instance types and sizes (overprovisioning pays for unused capacity, underprovisioning hurts performance — right-size from the start); purchasing model (prepaid vs on-demand changes long-term expense); tagging from day one; and monitoring early — cost alerts and usage dashboards set up at deployment avoid surprises.
Overprovisioning — and five counters
Acquiring more resources than your workload requires: unnecessary expense and inefficiency. Five strategies against it: right-size (analyze current usage; Trusted Advisor and Compute Optimizer recommend better fits); use reserved capacity only for steady workloads; leverage auto-scaling — scale up in peaks, down when idle; monitor utilization continuously (CPU, memory, storage trends); and implement cost governance — budgets, alerts, and regular allocation reviews.
Tags as cost infrastructure
Tags such as department, project, or cost center track ownership and accountability: allocate costs accurately across teams, quickly spot idle or misconfigured resources, support audits, and enforce cost governance. Apply them from day one, activate cost-allocation tags in the billing console, and the proactive approach streamlines cost management and keeps budget discipline.
Scaling strategies
Scaling adjusts resource capacity up or down to match current demand, protecting service quality (consistent response times under high demand), performance (efficient traffic distribution) and reliability (no overload-driven downtime). Manual scaling relies on forecasted demand and scheduled adjustments — slower to react, suited to predictable steady workloads; auto-scaling watches real-time metrics (CPU, network load) and responds rapidly to spikes or drops for variable workloads — but set limits (a maximum instance count) so scaling stays controlled and the bill cannot balloon.
Six overspending scenarios
Common ways bills derail, each with its mitigation: overprovisioned compute → right-size from the metrics; seasonal e-commerce peaks → auto-scaling up and down; steady rising demand → Savings Plans for discounted multi-service commitment; shared multi-team accounts → tags for allocation; cross-region data-transfer spikes → monitoring and alerts on the misconfigured flow; idle and unused resources → regular cost reviews that decommission them.
The standing cost playbook
Right-size resources (review usage regularly; terminate or downscale idle/underutilized assets); auto-scale with sensible limits; commit with Reserved Instances or Savings Plans for steady workloads; tag everything for ownership and usage; configure dashboards and alerts and act quickly on them; and run regular cost reviews with Cost Explorer and detailed billing reports, adjusting strategy from performance and usage assessments.

Quizzes

1. Name the five key AWS cost drivers.

Show answer
Compute (EC2, Lambda, container services), storage (S3, EBS, EFS), databases (RDS, DynamoDB), data transfer, and networking (VPC configuration, NAT gateways, load balancers).

2. What drives compute and storage costs?

Show answer
Compute: usage hours × instance type — premium types cost more. Storage: amount stored, access frequency, and storage class.

3. Which two services make up AWS's prepaid commitments, and what do you commit?

Show answer
Reserved Instances and Savings Plans — pay up front for a 1- or 3-year commitment (specific instance types for RIs, a $/hour usage level for SPs) in exchange for lower rates.

4. When do you choose Reserved Instances vs Savings Plans?

Show answer
RIs: predictable, steady workloads on a fixed instance shape — the deepest fixed-shape discount (up to 72%). SPs: savings across EC2, Fargate and Lambda regardless of family, size, OS or region (up to 72%) — the flexible default.

5. When is on-demand the right choice?

Show answer
Variable, unpredictable workloads (startups, fluctuating projects): pay per hour/GB/request, scale up or down quickly without overcommitting — highest flexibility, priciest per unit.

6. What does AWS Cost Explorer do for budgeting?

Show answer
Visualizes spending trends over time, breaks costs down by service/region/tag to isolate the biggest drivers, and forecasts and spots anomalies for smarter budgeting decisions.

7. Static threshold vs anomaly detection — what's the difference?

Show answer
Static: fires when spend crosses a value you set. Anomaly detection: machine learning flags unusual spending patterns even when no fixed threshold was crossed.

8. Name the five cost-impacting provisioning decisions.

Show answer
Region selection, instance types and sizes (right-sizing), purchasing model, tagging from day one, and monitoring early.

9. What are the five strategies to avoid overprovisioning?

Show answer
Right-size (metrics + Trusted Advisor / Compute Optimizer), reserve only for steady workloads, auto-scale, monitor utilization continuously, and enforce cost governance (budgets, alerts, allocation reviews).

10. One line: what does 'mitigation' mean for AWS overspending?

Show answer
Actively reducing, managing, and preventing unnecessary spending — identify cost inefficiencies and apply strategies (right-sizing, autoscaling, commitments, tags, alerts, regular reviews) to minimize their impact.

1. An online store sees heavy traffic during promotional events, then quiet weeks afterward.

Show answer
Auto-scaling: add compute during the spikes and scale back down when the event ends — no cost of running extra capacity off-peak.

2. A media streaming service expects demand to rise steadily over the next year across EC2, Fargate and Lambda.

Show answer
A (Compute) Savings Plan: discounted rates across all three services, aligned to forecasted usage — substantial savings and a more predictable cost structure.

3. A high-powered EC2 fleet is provisioned, but performance metrics show the workload needs far less.

Show answer
Right-size: use Trusted Advisor / Compute Optimizer recommendations and move to smaller instance types that handle the real traffic — reduced cost at the same performance.

4. A large organization shares one AWS account across many departments; nobody can say who spends what.

Show answer
Apply and activate cost-allocation tags (department/project/cost center), then group costs by tag in Cost Explorer — idle and overspending teams become visible and fixable.

5. A misconfigured application suddenly replicates data between regions and the bill jumps.

Show answer
Monitoring tools and alerts detect the anomaly for a timely review — correct the configuration and watch cross-region flows.

6. The monthly bill creeps up; old projects' EC2 instances and unattached volumes turn out to be still running.

Show answer
Regular cost reviews: decommission the idle resources — near-zero-effort, immediate waste reduction.

7. A team wants a forecast plus a 6-month service-by-service trend view before the budget meeting.

Show answer
Cost Explorer: monthly trend chart grouped by service category, forecast line, exported into the budget conversation.

8. A rendering job will consume huge compute tonight; the jobs can tolerate a 2-minute interruption.

Show answer
Spot Instances: spare capacity at up to 90% off, with checkpoint-and-retry handling the interruption notice.
