Exam-prep notes — reading AWS’s global infrastructure (Regions, Availability Zones, edge locations), navigating the same account+region context in Console and CloudShell, the four-box pre-deployment checklist, a public test web server launched on EC2, what a wrong-context deployment costs and how to restore the right one, and where the five service families live.

Summary

  • Global architecture — three categories to keep apart: a Region is a completely separate geographic area hosting multiple data centers (the greatest fault tolerance, and where-in-the-world you choose to run), an Availability Zone is an isolated, self-powered location inside one chosen Region (physically separated, low-latency links between), and edge locations are the data centers that serve users closest, working through points of presence. Verified from AWS today: 39 Regions / 124 AZs live, 2 more Regions announced (Saudi Arabia, Chile); every Region carries a minimum of three AZs; GovCloud (US) adds two US-sovereign Regions for government and compliance workloads.
  • AZ names vs AZ IDs — AZ names (us-west-2a) are account-relative labels; the AZ ID (use1-az4-style) names the same physical location in every account — pin multi-account zonal designs on IDs, never names. Console mechanics: login lands in a default region (us-east-1), the picker lists only enabled regions (admins scope them for compliance), and switching the view region hides everything deployed elsewhere — VPCs and their subnets are regional. CloudShell is the same world by CLI with the same credentials (sts get-caller-identity), bound the same way.
  • The pre-deployment checklist — four boxes before touching any service: account (test workloads in a test account — an account is a hard boundary that minimizes blast radius), region (closest to your users; every request pays the round trip), service (requirements decide — OS-level customization demands EC2, not a fully managed service), service availability (not every service or feature exists in every region — verify first). The sample server went live on EC2: default VPC (learning only), auto-assigned public IP, inbound-HTTP security-group rule, user-data script installing the web server, no key pair because nothing connected to it.
  • Wrong context, priced — AWS never warns; a wrong region just works. Operationally: instances “vanish” (the view pointed elsewhere), recreating them duplicates resources, latency climbs, data-residency rules break, and regional services stop finding their partners. In billing: identical specs bill differently per region — t3.medium ~$0.042/h in us-east-1 vs ~$0.067/h in sa-east-1 (on-demand, Linux, Shared; verified 2026-11-02) — and cross-region and even cross-AZ traffic is metered.
  • Fixing and framing — restore the right context by creating an AMI (captures the attached volume) or a plain EBS snapshot and copying it across regions/accounts, or by redeploying an IaC template (Terraform, Pulumi, CloudFormation). Frame everything through the two boundaries: the five service families — compute (EC2/Lambda/ECS), storage (EBS regional with mobile snapshots; S3 buckets parked in one Region, names global-unique in the partition plus the new opt-in account regional namespaces with the -an suffix), networking (VPC regional; overlapping CIDRs block connected VPCs), identity (IAM global inside one account, never cross-accounts by default), monitoring with regional CloudWatch log groups best centralized — and when a resource seems gone: check account → region → IAM permission before believing it.

Main concepts

The three infrastructure categories
Regions — a completely separate geographic area hosting multiple data centers: the greatest possible fault tolerance and stability, and the decision of where your workloads in the world live. Availability Zones — isolated, independent locations inside one chosen Region, each with its own redundant power and networking, physically separated to reduce a disaster’s impact yet low-latency connected to each other. Edge locations — data centers that help deliver services with the absolute lowest latency: closer to end users than Regions or AZs, operating through points of presence.
The region footprint today (verified 2026-11-02)
AWS’s global-infrastructure page shows 124 Availability Zones within 39 Geographic Regions, with 7 more AZs and 2 more Regions announced (Saudi Arabia, Chile) — live Regions span every populated continent: North and South America, Europe, Africa, Australia and beyond. The dots also include GovCloud (US): two US-sovereign Regions (US-East, US-West) dedicated to government workloads and their strict compliance requirements, operated by US citizens on US soil, verified-US access.
Availability zones: names vs IDs
Naming: region code + letter (us-west-2a…2d — that Region carries four AZs today). Names are account-relative: in the oldest Regions the AZ names mapped per account — us-east-1a in your account may not be us-east-1a in another. AWS’s current docs: every Region launched after November 2012 uses a uniform mapping, and accounts created from November 2025 get uniform mappings in the legacy Regions too — yet coordination across accounts should still key on the AZ ID (use1-az4-style): unique and consistent, naming the same physical location in every account. The console’s subnet panel lists AZ IDs use1-az1…use1-az6 — us-east-1’s six, with one more (Maryland) announced for 2026.
Finding your way in the console
Console home lands in a default region — us-east-1 (North Virginia), upper right. The region picker lists only ENABLED regions — account/organization admins enable or disable regions for compliance or business requirements. Click a region and you work inside its data centers; every entry follows the naming syntax (ap-southeast-1, eu-west-1, us-west-2…), and subnet panels expose the AZ ID beside the name — exactly where cross-account designs need it.
Why regional matters: the view trap
Services deployed in one region do not show up in another: the VPC seen in us-east-1 simply is not there when the console views us-west-2 — not hidden, not grayed out, nowhere in the menu. A VPC is a virtual private cloud — an isolated networking slice for your account — and it is regional, along with everything inside it (subnets, route tables, gateways). Switch the region selector and the resource list changes with it.
CloudShell — the second view
Same platform, other interface: CloudShell gives a CLI view of your account with your current credentials — aws sts get-caller-identity prints the identity (the same cloud_user ARN as the console session). The CLI session follows the console’s region: aws ec2 describe-vpcs in us-east-1 shows that region’s VPCs; switch to us-west-2 and the same command returns a different world. The region you point at decides what you see — GUI and CLI alike.
The requirements scene
You are a cloud engineer at a small startup (small team, limited budget, no room for over-engineering — whatever gets built must be simple and deliberate). Users are almost entirely in the Pacific Northwest of the US — geography is one of the biggest deployment decisions; where users physically sit should decide where infrastructure sits. The need: a sample web server for simple testing (not production — no revenue depends on this box). It must be publicly reachable over HTTP and HTTPS, and it must allow customizing the operating system — installing packages, tweaking settings, configuring firewalls — which rules out handing it to a fully managed service where AWS controls the OS.
The checklist in action
Account: a sample test server → the test account, because organizations separate dev/staging/prod across accounts: an account is a hard boundary for better isolation and separation, minimizing your blast radius — one of the most fundamental AWS habits. Region: Pacific Northwest users → us-west-2 (Oregon) — closest to the primary user group, since every browser request physically travels to the resource and back: farther means slower. Service: the OS-customization requirement → Amazon EC2: an instance managed like any other VM — you choose the OS, connect, install and configure whatever the team needs, publicly reachable when implemented right. Availability: EC2 is a core service available essentially everywhere — but a more complex companion service may not exist in your region; those 30-60 seconds of verification save hundreds of build hours of rework.
Deploying the validated workload
In the console: switch the region picker to Oregon (us-west-2). EC2 → Launch instance: name it; keep the default Amazon Linux AMI and instance type. Key pair: proceed without (nothing connects to this box — keys are for SSH/RDP). Networking in a fresh sandbox: create the default VPC — internet access plus subnets spread over AZs — refresh the picker and select it, with the caveat: default VPCs are for initial testing/learning only; create your own VPC as best practice. Subnet/AZ: No preference (a chosen subnet pins the instance to that subnet’s AZ). Auto-assign public IP: enabled for the public-internet requirement. Firewall: new security group + inbound rule Type HTTP (port/protocol auto-filled) with source Anywhere. Advanced → User data: paste a small ec2_user_data.sh shell script — installs and configures the web server at OS level. Launch; wait the fast-forwarded ~30 seconds; Open address over HTTP — the sample server, publicly reachable, launched in the right region (the AZ shown confirms it).
Symptoms of wrong context
The consequences separate into two families. Operational: the vanished resource — console shows nothing, no error, no trace (you are pointed at the wrong region, deployed or now); recreating the “missing” one creates duplicates across regions — the original still lives in its home region; latency — every request takes the longer physical round trip; data residency and compliance — data living where it is not permitted: not an inconvenience but potentially a fine; service connectivity — many regional services expect their partners in the same region, and a lot of them do not support cross-region connectivity. Billing: the same resource can cost different amounts in different regions — identical t3.medium specs bill ~$0.042/h in us-east-1 vs ~$0.067/h in sa-east-1 (Linux, Shared, on-demand; verified 2026-11-02); duplicates quietly bill; cross-region and even cross-AZ data transfer is metered — moved data is never free.
Why AWS stays silent about it
Regional isolation is deliberate architectural choice, not a bug or a limitation — it keeps a problem in one part of the world from cascading into another, and it is one of the things that makes AWS resilient. The flip side: AWS happily lets you deploy anywhere in your accounts and treats every region as a first-class, fully valid deployment target — for someone somewhere in the world it truly is. No warning dialogue asks “are you sure?”, and no guardrail questions a team in Oregon deploying in Frankfurt. Nothing failed, so nothing alerted — a wrong-region deployment is not an error to AWS; it is on you.
Restoring the right context
The junior engineer believed they launched in us-east-1; their default region had actually been set to us-west-2 — so us-east-1 showed no instances and the running server sat in us-west-2 (opened over HTTP, AZ confirming the region). Moves to restore: (1) create an AMI — Actions → Image and templates → create image: it snapshots the attached volume, and can be shared/copied across regions and accounts, then used to deploy in the correct region; (2) snapshot the volume itself — Storage → the EBS volume → create snapshot: the OS and EC2 layer left alone, the data copied cross-region or cross-account; (3) infrastructure as code — Terraform, Pulumi, CloudFormation templates describe the deployment, so re-deploying it in the right region is a button. Each path is explored on its own — this is the briefing, not the deep dive.
Mapping the five families to their scopes
Instead of dozens of services, five families. Compute (EC2, Lambda, ECS): regional — the instance launched in us-east-1 existed exactly and only there; Lambda functions and ECS containers likewise. Storage: EBS volumes are regional — a disk next to the instance it attaches to — but snapshots travel: take one, copy it across regions, recreate the data elsewhere. S3: every bucket physically lives in one Region you choose; the twist is the name — the shared global namespace made bucket names unique across every account in the partition, and the newer account regional namespace (suffix -accountId-region-an) now reserves names for your account alone — no other account can create them, ever. Networking: the VPC and the pieces inside it (subnets, route tables, gateways) are regional; planning multi-account, multi-region IP space matters because VPCs with overlapping CIDRs cannot be connected. IAM: global — no region picker, users and roles defined once for the entire account and usable in every supported region — but still respecting account boundaries: your users/roles exist nowhere else; and global identities acting on regional resources can reach further than intended — always plan least privilege. Monitoring/log groups: CloudWatch collects metrics, stores logs, raises alarms — regionally: a log group defined in us-east-1 exists only there; plan it and centralize logging (e.g. push all logs to one S3 bucket for security/ops teams).
Accounts: the other half of “where”
Regions are only half the answer to where a resource lives; every resource also lives inside exactly one account — the account that created it. Other accounts cannot see across into yours by default. Access can be provided, but deliberately: explicit permissions must be granted in BOTH accounts for cross-account access — the boundary stays solid until both sides agree to open it. One-sided trust is not enough, so cross-account access never happens by accident.
Four takeaways and the troubleshooting trio
(1) Billing — the same instance costs different amounts in different regions; misplaced and duplicated resources bill quietly where nobody is watching — the region choice is a financial decision, not only architectural. (2) Users — closer resources generally respond faster; far-away ones tax every request. (3) IAM — principals (users, roles) need explicit permissions granted in both accounts for cross-account access; the account stays solid until both sides open it. (4) Where did my resources go? — they usually have not gone anywhere: check the account being used, the region chosen and shown in the picker, and IAM permissions to see the resource — that trio explains nearly every vanished resource you will ever chase. Show answer
Key the design on AZ IDs — AZ names map per-account, but an AZ ID (e.g. use1-az2) is the same physical location in both accounts. Find each account’s name→ID mapping (describe-availability-zones or the RAM pane) and reference IDs everywhere.

6. During an incident, your security team is chasing logs across every region’s CloudWatch.

Show answer
Centralize: push all logs to a single S3 bucket (one security/ops bucket for all accounts). Regional log groups exist in their region alone — the centralized location makes incident-chasing one-pane work instead of a region-by-region hunt.

7. Your IaC wants bucket names it can rely on forever — no race against every other AWS account for the same name.

Show answer
Create buckets in the account regional namespace: name prefix-accountId-region-an (console auto-adds the suffix; CLI/API via the x-amz-bucket-namespace: account-regional CreateBucket header). Only your account can ever create names bearing your account’s suffix; other accounts’ requests are rejected.

Where does it live? — scope drill

For each resource, pick its scope — first try counts.

Answered 0 of 8 · First-try correct: 0

A VPC — and everything inside it: subnets, route tables, gateways.

Region — the VPC is an isolated networking slice of exactly one region; its internals live there too.

A single subnet that holds your instances.

One AZ — a subnet exists inside exactly one AZ; choosing it at launch pins the instance into that AZ.

An EBS volume attached to an instance.

One AZ — the volume must sit in the same region and AZ as the instance; regional family, zonal placement.

An IAM user or role acting on resources.

Global (account) — no region picker: users/roles are defined once for the account, and exist nowhere else.

A CloudWatch log group your alarm reads.

Region — a log group defined in us-east-1 exists only there; plan and centralize logging deliberately.

An S3 bucket (placement — the name is a different story).

Region — the bucket physically lives in the one region chosen at creation; the NAME follows the global-namespace rule instead.

A Lambda function serving your endpoint.

Region — the compute families live in the region where you created them, and only there.

A fresh AMI, before you copy it anywhere.

Region — the image exists in the region it was created in; share/copy it across regions or accounts to deploy elsewhere.
